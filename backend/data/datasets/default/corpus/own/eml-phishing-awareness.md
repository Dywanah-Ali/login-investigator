# How to Spot a Phishing Email

## Message

From: Isabel Knight (isabel.knight), Security, isabel.knight@corp.example

To: All Staff

Sent: 2026-08-19 at 10:00 UTC

Subject: Pause before responding to an unexpected email

## Check the request

A phishing email tries to make you do something useful to the sender, such as disclose a password, open an attachment or approve a sign-in. It may look routine and may appear to come from someone you know. Good spelling and a familiar display name are not proof that a message is safe.

Look at the request as well as the sender. Unexpected urgency, instructions to keep something secret, a changed payment arrangement or a demand to bypass the usual approval process are reasons to pause. Ask whether the request makes sense for your role and whether you were expecting the attachment or information.

If you need to confirm a request, contact the person through details you already know. Do not use a new phone number supplied only in the message you are checking. Do not reply with sensitive information while you are still unsure.

## Passwords and MFA

Never enter a password after clicking a link in an email. Open the usual company sign-in route yourself. A page that looks familiar may still be asking for information it should not receive. Support will not ask you to send your password, an authenticator code or a recovery code.

Never approve an MFA request you did not start. Repeated prompts do not make the request legitimate. Deny the request and contact Security through the normal route. T1621 is a reference Security may use for attempts to obtain approval through repeated MFA requests; staff only need to remember to approve their own sign-ins.

## Reporting a message

Send a report to Security through the established company contact route. Include the sender, subject and time received in UTC. Explain whether you clicked anything, entered information, opened an attachment or approved a request. Security will explain how to provide the message safely if it needs further detail.

Do not circulate the email widely or ask colleagues to test its links. If you already responded, tell Security promptly and describe what you did. Clear information is more useful than guessing whether the message caused harm. The helpdesk can assist with ordinary access problems while Security assesses a suspicious request.
